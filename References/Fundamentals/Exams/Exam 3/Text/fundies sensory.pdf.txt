                     Sensory Alterations
• Humans have five basic senses
   • Sight
   • Smell
   • Hearing
   • Taste
   • Touch
• The inability to see, smell, hear, taste, or feel are referred to as sensory
  alterations.

          Three Common Sensory Alterations
    • Sensory Deficits
    • Sensory Deprivation
    • Sensory Overload




                                                                                                            4
                            Copyright 2022 Assessment Technologies Institute, L.L.C. All rights reserved.




A sensory deﬁcit occurs when a client has a deﬁcit in the expected function
of one or more of their ﬁve senses. For example, a client may not be able
to receive certain stimuli that allows them to process sounds below a
certain decibel level, leading to a hearing deﬁcit.

Sensory deﬁcits can occur due to injury, illness, or aging and can have
signiﬁcant eﬀects on a client’s general well-being and daily functioning.

Sensory deprivation occurs when there is a reduction in or absence of
stimuli to one or more of the ﬁve senses.

An example of sensory deprivation is blindness, which causes an absence
of visual sensory stimulation.

Sensory overload is deﬁned as receiving stimuli at a rate and intensity
beyond the brain’s ability to process the stimuli in a meaningful way. This
can cause the client to feel anxious, restless, and confused.

For example, a client who is critically ill and frequently in pain may be
receiving multiple sensory stimuli, both from their own body and the busy
care environment: room lights, noises from monitors and other
equipment, and frequent visits by nurses and providers.

                                Sensory Deprivation
     Sensory Deprivation:
     • Insufficient meaningful stimulation — common in hospitalized patients

         • Who is at risk: ICU patients, patients in private rooms with limited visitors, cognitively
           impaired older adults, patients with prolonged bedrest
         • What it looks like: Confusion, anxiety, hallucinations, depression, altered time perception


     • Nursing interventions:
         • Encourage family presence and meaningful interaction
         • Provide clock and calendar — orient to day and time regularly
         • Encourage meaningful activity — TV, music, reading, conversation
         • Maintain day/night light cycles — lights up during the day, dimmed at night



                                                                                                                     5
                                     Copyright 2022 Assessment Technologies Institute, L.L.C. All rights reserved.




ICU delirium — which has sensory deprivation and overload as contributing factors —
affects up to 80% of mechanically ventilated patients and is associated with longer
ICU stays, worse cognitive outcomes, and higher mortality. The ABCDEF bundle
(Assess, prevent and manage pain; Both spontaneous awakening and breathing trials;
Choice of analgesia and sedation; Delirium assessment; Early mobility; Family
engagement) specifically addresses sensory factors. Even in a general hospital setting,
the nurse who actively manages stimulation — maintaining day/night cycles,
clustering care, reducing alarm noise — is preventing delirium and improving patient
outcomes.

A patient with hearing loss (deficit) stops receiving meaningful auditory input from
their environment (deprivation). A patient who is blind (deficit) is deprived of all
visual stimuli (deprivation). The deficit is the broken receiver. The deprivation is what
the brain experiences as a result.

                                   Sensory Overload
     Sensory Overload:
     • Excessive or uncontrollable stimulation — also common in hospitalized patients

         • Who is at risk: ICU patients, post-operative patients, patients with pain, patients with anxiety
           or PTSD
         • What it looks like: Confusion, agitation, inability to rest, heightened pain perception, anxiety


     • Nursing interventions:
         • Consolidate nursing care to reduce interruptions
         • Silence unnecessary alarms — respond promptly to reduce alarm fatigue for patient
         • Use a quiet, calm tone of voice
         • Offer eye mask and earplugs at night
         • Close the door when possible

                                    Vision Impairment
     • Most common sensory deficit in older adults — and frequently underassessed


     • Types:
         • Presbyopia
             • age-related loss of near vision (reading)
         • Cataracts
             • clouding of the lens from clumping of protein; gradual vision blurring
         • Glaucoma
             • increased intraocular pressure damaging the optic nerve; peripheral vision lost first
         • Macular degeneration
             • central vision loss; common in older adults
         • Diabetic retinopathy
             • damage from chronic hyperglycemia




                                                                                                                       9
                                       Copyright 2022 Assessment Technologies Institute, L.L.C. All rights reserved.




Vision impairment significantly increases fall risk in hospitalized patients. A patient
who cannot see the IV pole, the edge of the bed, or the call light button is at
immediate safety risk. The clock position system for describing food orientation is a
practical, dignity-preserving technique that students can use immediately. Patients
are often reluctant to report vision difficulty — the nurse who assesses proactively,
particularly with older adults and diabetic patients, will identify needs that would
otherwise go unmet.
                                  Vision Impairment
     • Nursing interventions:
         • Orient patient to room layout verbally and physically on admission
         • Describe food on tray using clock positions: "potatoes at 3 o'clock, green beans at 9 o'clock"
         • Ensure adequate lighting
         • Use large print for written materials; offer verbal alternatives
         • Keep call light, glasses, and personal items within reach
         • Do not rearrange the room without informing the patient
         • Ambulate safely
         • Assess glasses: clean lenses, proper fit, patient has them available

     • Expressive (nonfluent)
     • Receptive
     • Global




                                                                                                            11
                            Copyright 2022 Assessment Technologies Institute, L.L.C. All rights reserved.




Expressive
Inability to name common objects or express ideas in words or writing

 Also known as nonﬂuent aphasia and is due to damage to the Broca area
located in the frontal lobe of the brain. A client who has nonﬂuent aphasia
may understand speech but be unable to speak the words they want to
say. The client may speak in short phrases that the listener can
understand, such as “hungry now”. Clients who have expressive aphasia
are often easily frustrated as they are aware of their speech diﬃculty.


A client who has comprehensive aphasia speaks in long sentences that
have no meaning and often include unnecessary and made-up words.
Comprehensive aphasia also causes a client to not understand what is
being said and they are unable to comprehend that others can’t
understand what they are saying.

Receptive
Inability to understand written or spoken language




                                                                                                                 11
Global
Inability to understand language or communicate orally

causes severe impairments in communication. Global aphasia is caused by
signiﬁcant damage to various language areas of the brain. Clients who
have this type of aphasia have poor comprehension of language and may
be unable to form words or sentences.

Patients with aphasia have varied degrees of inability to speak, interpret, or
understand language.
Expressive aphasia, a motor type of aphasia, is the inability to name common objects
or express simple ideas in words or writing. For example, a patient understands a
question but is unable to express an answer.
Receptive aphasia is the inability to understand written or spoken language. A patient
is able to express words but is unable to understand questions or comments of
others.
Global aphasia is the inability to understand language or communicate orally.

Aphasia: Types and Communication Approaches

  • Expressive Aphasia (Broca's):
      • Patient understands language but cannot produce words fluently
      • Speech is halting, effortful, non-fluent
      • Patient is often acutely aware of the deficit and frustrated
      • Example: "Pain... leg... bad" (trying to say "I have bad pain in my leg")
      • Approach: Yes/no questions, picture boards, letter boards, patience (never finish their
        sentences)

The most critical point about aphasia is that it is a language disorder, not a cognitive
disorder. A patient with expressive aphasia may be fully oriented and cognitively
intact but unable to express it — treating them as cognitively impaired is both
clinically inaccurate and harmful to dignity. Speech therapy should be involved early.
The nurse's role includes documenting which communication strategies work for that
specific patient and communicating this to every team member so the patient
doesn't have to re-establish communication methods with each new caregiver.

  • Receptive Aphasia (Wernicke's):
      • Patient produces fluent speech that may not make sense
      • Patient has difficulty understanding spoken or written language
      • "I was going to the blanket table before the curtain came in" (fluent but nonsensical —
        called word salad)
      • "You know that smoodle pinkered and then I want to get the help for the freezles, but it
        doesn't make any track.” (fluent but contains made up words)
      • Following commands may be inconsistent or absent
      • Approach: Simple short sentences, gestures, demonstration — verbal reassurance may not
        be comprehended

      • Global Aphasia:
          • Both expression and comprehension severely impaired
          • Most severe form — typically from large dominant hemisphere stroke
          • Example: may repeat one word (yes, da, etc.) or may be completely silent
          • Approach: Nonverbal communication, consistent caregivers, calm environment,

          • Involve speech therapy early

          • Avoid saying “I know what you mean” unless you truly do!

A client who has tactile hypersensitivity has a peripheral and/or central
nervous system that is overly sensitive to sensory input, speciﬁcally tactile
stimuli. The client may be unable to wear clothing that has a zipper or a
shirt that has long sleeves.

 A client who has tactile hyposensitivity may not be able to feel extremes in
temperature or have a limited ability to feel pain from bone fractures or
lacerations. These clients may seek out tactile stimulation by frequently
touching objects in their environment.

 Peripheral neuropathy occurs following damage to or malfunction of a
peripheral nerve. Peripheral neuropathy most often aﬀects the feet and
hands, but it can occur in other parts of the body. Manifestations include
weakness, numbness, burning, tingling, pain, inability to detect
temperature, and hyporeﬂexia. Two other types of neuropathy include
idiopathic neuropathy and diabetic neuropathy.

Diabetic neuropathy is nerve damage that occurs in clients who have
diabetes mellitus. Diabetic neuropathy occurs over time from high blood




                                                                                                                  15
glucose levels and high levels of triglycerides in the blood, which can cause
damage to the nerves and to the small blood vessels supplying blood to
the nerves.

                    Communication Adaptations
     • Hearing Impairment:
        • Face the patient directly — lip reading is a key compensatory strategy
        • Speak clearly at a normal pace — shouting distorts lip patterns and does not help
          sensorineural loss
        • Reduce background noise before speaking
        • Ensure hearing aids are in and functioning
        • Provide written instructions for all key information
        • Use interpreter services for sign language when needed — never use family as interpreter for
          clinical information


     • Vision Impairment:
        • Always identify yourself when entering the room: "Mrs. Park, it's your nurse Jamie"
        • Describe what you are doing before you do it
        • Use large print materials; offer to read written materials aloud
        • Ensure glasses are clean and available before patient needs them

                                                                                                                   17
                                   Copyright 2022 Assessment Technologies Institute, L.L.C. All rights reserved.




The hearing aid maintenance point is practically important and often overlooked: a
hearing aid that is not functioning is no better than no hearing aid. Wax guards clog,
batteries die, and devices need cleaning. The nurse who checks hearing aid function
as part of the admission assessment and ensures the patient has batteries available
will significantly improve the patient's ability to participate in their own care. The
instruction never to use family members as clinical interpreters is a patient safety and
legal standard — family members may omit, soften, or alter information, and the
dynamic of a family member interpreting medical information creates ethical
complications around privacy and consent.
