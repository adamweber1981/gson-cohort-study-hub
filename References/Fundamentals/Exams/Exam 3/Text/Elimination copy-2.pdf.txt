                  Types of Urinary Incontinence
    Stress incontinence: coughing, sneezing, laughing, or physical activity that increases
    pressure on the bladder
    Urge incontinence: strong urge to urinate, but leaking occurs before getting to the toilet

    Reflex incontinence: urinary leakage as a result of nerve damage

    Overflow incontinence: incomplete bladder emptying which results in the bladder
    overfilling when full
    Functional incontinence: physical inability to reach the toilet in time

    Bedwetting (nocturnal enuresis)

Urinary incontinence (UI) is the inability to control urination, resulting in
the involuntary passage of urine. It can be caused by many factors. This
common bladder control problem affects the retention, or holding, and
releasing of urine from the bladder.

Urinary retention is a condition in which the bladder does not completely
empty with urination. Health problems such as prostate enlargement or a
cystocele (prolapsed bladder) can prevent urine from leaving the bladder
fully.

If not addressed, urinary retention can cause urinary tract infections,
bladder damage, kidney damage, and urinary incontinence.

Intravenous Pyelogram (IVP)
Contrast dye is injected intravenously, filtered by the kidneys, and excreted into the
urinary tract. X-rays are taken in sequence as the contrast moves through the kidneys,
ureters, and bladder, creating an image of the entire upper and lower urinary tract.
Used to identify kidney stones, obstruction, tumors, or structural abnormalities.

Retrograde Pyelogram:
Contrast is injected directly into the ureter via a cystoscope (going up, or retrograde,
rather than through the bloodstream). Used when IV contrast is contraindicated or
when better visualization of the ureters or renal pelvis is needed. Often performed by
urology.

Urinalysis is a lab test commonly used by health care providers to evaluate
for a variety of disorders, including bladder infections or UTIs, kidney
infections, kidney disease, and diabetes. This test includes visual
examination of the urine, dipstick testing, and microscopic examination.

Clean catch - patient voids and discards a small amount of urine; continues voiding in
a sterile specimen container to collect the urine; stops voiding into container;




                                                                                                                                    12
removes container and continues voiding; then discards the last amount of urine in
the bladder. The first small amount of urine voided helps to flush away any organisms
near the meatus because the findings may be inaccurate if these organisms enter the
specimen. In addition, it is generally thought that urine voided at midstream is most
characteristic of the urine the body is producing.

Sterile urine specimens may be obtained by catheterizing the patient’s bladder or by
taking the specimen from an indwelling catheter already in place

Random means just that – a random sample at anytime

Urine culture - A urine culture is used to evaluate urine for the presence of
bacteria and yeast that may cause a UTI. This test is commonly ordered in
addition to a urinalysis to confirm the presence of bacteria in urine
revealed on the urine reagent strip.

24 hour - Another diagnostic test for the urinary system is 24-hour urine
collection, in which urine is collected over a 24-hour period, placed in a
special container, and refrigerated. The client can collect the urine at home
after the nurse reviews how to perform the procedure. This test allows the
provider to evaluate the amounts of chemicals that are in the urine over a
24-hour period and can be helpful in determining kidney function.

Constipation occurs when a client has infrequent bowel movements, often
defined as fewer than three bowel movements in a week. Additionally,
these stools are hard, lumpy, and difficult to pass.

Manifestations that require medical attention include fever, bleeding from
the GI tract, abdominal pain, vomiting, low back pain, and weight loss. If
constipation is chronic, fecal impaction or obstruction can occur.

Interventions to treat constipation include a high-fiber diet, staying well
hydrated, exercise, bowel training, and medications to soften stools.

It can be acute, lasting about 1 to 2 days; persistent, lasting longer than 2
weeks but less than 4 weeks; or chronic, lasting longer than 4 weeks.

If diarrhea is persistent, dehydration and malabsorption can occur, and
both can be life-threatening if severe. In adults, symptoms that require
medical follow-up would include fever of 102°F or higher, diarrhea lasting
longer than 2 days or six or more bowel movements a day, abdominal
pain, or the presence of blood or black feces.

Interventions are aimed at removing irritating factors and ensuring
rehydration. If the client is experiencing an acute case of diarrhea, over-
the-counter medications such as loperamide or bismuth subsalicylate may
be used. If GI infection is the cause, antibiotics and/or probiotics will be
prescribed to help restore the intestinal flora and re-create the usual GI
environment.

Tap water is hypotonic and exerts an osmotic pressure lower than fluid in interstitial
spaces. After infusion into the colon, tap water escapes from the bowel lumen into
interstitial spaces. The net movement of water is low. The infused volume stimulates
defecation before large amounts of water leave the bowel. Use caution if ordered to
repeat tap-water enemas because water toxicity or circulatory overload could
develop if the body absorbs large amounts of water.

Physiologically, normal saline is the safest solution to use because it exerts the same
osmotic pressure as fluids in interstitial spaces surrounding the bowel. The volume of
infused saline stimulates peristalsis. Giving saline enemas lessens the danger of
excess fluid absorption.

Hypertonic solutions (fleets) infused into the bowel exert osmotic pressure that pulls
fluids out of interstitial spaces. The colon fills with fluid and the resultant distention
promotes defecation. Patients unable to tolerate large volumes of fluid benefit most
from this type of enema, which is by design low volume. This type of enema is
contraindicated for patients who are dehydrated and young infants. The commercially
prepared Fleet enema is the most common.




                                                                                                                               24
You add soapsuds to tap water or saline to create the effect of intestinal irritation to
stimulate peristalsis. Use only pure castile soap that comes in a liquid form that is
included in most soapsuds enema kits. Use soapsuds enemas with caution in
pregnant women and older adults because they could cause electrolyte imbalance or
damage to the intestinal mucosa.

Oil-retention enemas lubricate the feces in the rectum and colon. The feces absorb
the oil and become softer and easier to pass. To enhance action of the oil, the patient
retains the enema for several hours if possible.
