Hypokalemia – Treatment and Nursing Care
SPEAKER NOTES FOR SLIDE 16:
IV potassium safety is one of the highest-stakes nursing responsibilities in electrolyte management and deserves emphasis. Potassium chloride is a high-alert medication — it appears on the Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) list of medications that have caused patient death when misused. IV potassium administered as a push or at too high a concentration causes immediate cardiac arrest. Many facilities have removed concentrated potassium from floor stock entirely and require pharmacy preparation of all potassium infusions for exactly this reason. The pain at the IV site is a real clinical issue — potassium is caustic to veins and patients frequently complain of burning during infusion. Slowing the rate slightly and ensuring adequate dilution helps, but pain is expected and should be communicated to the patient in advance. The relationship between potassium and magnesium is clinically important: hypokalemia is very difficult to correct when hypomagnesemia is present simultaneously because magnesium is needed for the cellular mechanism that maintains potassium balance. Always check magnesium when treating hypokalemia.

The most common causes of hypercalcemia are hyperparathyroidism and cancer. Both of these conditions result in excessive calcium being drawn from the bones and into the bloodstream. In hyperparathyroidism, the parathyroid glands secrete excessive amount of the hormone PTH. The elevated PTH levels in the body then lead to increased absorption of calcium by the intestines, increased reabsorption by the kidneys, and increased bone resorption—a process that ultimately leads to excessive levels of calcium in the blood.

Hypercalcemia – Treatment and Nursing Care
SPEAKER NOTES FOR SLIDE 22:
The mnemonic for hypercalcemia symptoms is Bones, Groans, Moans, and Psychic Overtones — bone pain, GI symptoms (nausea, constipation, abdominal pain), muscle weakness, and neurological/psychiatric symptoms (confusion, depression, lethargy). Hypercalcemia of malignancy is one of the most common metabolic emergencies in cancer patients — it occurs when tumors either produce PTH-related peptide that mobilizes calcium from bone, or when bone metastases directly destroy bone and release calcium into the bloodstream. The nurse who sees a cancer patient with new confusion, nausea, and constipation should have hypercalcemia on their differential. Immobility is both a cause and a complication — bedbound patients continuously lose calcium from bone into the bloodstream, which is why encouraging ambulation is a direct treatment intervention and not just general care.

The most common cause of hypermagnesemia is acute or chronic kidney disease, as the impaired kidneys fail to excrete enough magnesium through the urine. While hypermagnesemia is not likely to occur from dietary intake, underlying bowel conditions can cause decreased gastrointestinal motility and lead to increased magnesium absorption. These conditions can be exacerbated by treatment with opioids or anticholinergics—such medications increase the dwell time of food boluses in the intestines, allowing for greater absorption of electrolytes such as magnesium. Laxatives and antacids containing magnesium may also cause hypermagnesemia if intake is excessive, as can as use of psychotropic medications containing lithium.

Hypermagnesemia – Treatment and Nursing Care
SPEAKER NOTES FOR SLIDE 29:
Deep tendon reflex assessment is the most critical nursing monitoring tool in hypermagnesemia — more immediately useful than waiting for lab results. The sequence of progression with rising magnesium levels follows a predictable pattern: decreased DTRs appear first, then hypotension and flushing, then respiratory depression, then cardiac arrest. Loss of the patellar reflex (knee jerk) is the clinical warning that magnesium levels are approaching dangerous territory. This is particularly relevant for patients receiving therapeutic IV magnesium for preeclampsia or eclampsia — obstetric nurses monitor DTRs continuously during magnesium infusions for exactly this reason. Calcium gluconate is the antidote and should be at the bedside of any patient on IV magnesium. This is a high-alert medication requiring double-checking with a second

Hypomagnesemia – Treatment and Nursing Care
SPEAKER NOTES FOR SLIDE 31:
The relationship between magnesium, potassium, and calcium is clinically critical and frequently tested. Magnesium is required for the sodium-potassium ATPase pump to function — without adequate magnesium the kidneys cannot retain potassium regardless of how much replacement is given. A patient with concurrent hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia who receives potassium replacement but not magnesium will continue to waste potassium renally. Correcting magnesium first — or simultaneously — is essential. The same relationship exists with calcium: PTH secretion and function requires magnesium. Patients on long-term PPIs are at particular risk for hypomagnesemia because PPIs reduce magnesium absorption in the gut — this is a FDA warning that was issued in 2011 and applies to all PPIs at prescription doses used for more than one year.
